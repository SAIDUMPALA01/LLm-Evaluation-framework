\### 4.3 Experiment 3 — Summary Consistency Test



To test whether the Consistency dimension extends beyond document generation into summarisation tasks, I used ChatGPT to summarise the same paragraph three times in three completely separate sessions with no conversation history between them.



The paragraph I used described artificial intelligence changing how people work, including automation of tasks like emails and data analysis, productivity improvements, concerns about job displacement and content reliability, and expert disagreement about long-term impact. I used the same prompt in every session: "Summarise this paragraph in exactly 5 bullet points."



I ran all three sessions on 7 May 2026. Before running the experiment I expected the results to be mostly similar because summarisation is a more constrained task than CV generation — there is less room for variation when the input is a fixed paragraph.



The results were more interesting than I expected.



All three sessions gave exactly 5 bullet points as instructed, which shows strong specification adherence. All three sessions used only information from the paragraph and added nothing external, showing strong contextual utilisation. Factual accuracy was high across all sessions.



However Session 1 was structurally different from Sessions 2 and 3 in a meaningful way. Session 1 split the automation examples into two separate bullets — one about companies using AI to automate tasks generally, and one specifically listing the examples of emails, data analysis, and reports. This left only one bullet to cover both job displacement concerns and expert disagreement, and as a result the expert disagreement point was weakened. Session 1 wrote "concerns exist about job losses, content reliability, and long-term effects" — combining three ideas into one bullet without clearly expressing that experts specifically disagree about the long-term impact.



Sessions 2 and 3 handled this differently. Both kept the automation examples within a single bullet, which freed up space for separate bullets on job concerns and expert disagreement. Session 2 wrote "experts have different opinions on AI's long-term impact" and Session 3 wrote "experts have differing opinions on AI's long-term impact" — both clearly expressing the disagreement that Session 1 had softened.



This structural difference has a real consequence for understanding. A user who only received Session 1 would read the summary and understand that there are general concerns about AI's future. A user who received Session 2 or Session 3 would understand that experts actively disagree — which is a stronger and more precise claim. Both users would believe they had received the complete and correct summary, because ChatGPT presented each answer with equal confidence and no warning that the structure or emphasis might vary.
